What Is a Non-Custodial (Self-Custody) Wallet?

A non-custodial wallet is one where you — not a company — hold the private keys that control your crypto. That single difference changes who can move your funds, who can freeze them, and who is responsible if something goes wrong.

Custodial vs. non-custodial

When you keep crypto on a centralized exchange, the exchange holds the private keys on your behalf. Your account balance is really an IOU: the exchange owes you that amount, and can move, freeze, or lose it — intentionally or through a hack, insolvency, or a mistake on their end.

A non-custodial (self-custody) wallet generates and stores your private keys directly on your own device. No company sits between you and your funds. Every transaction is signed locally, by you, before it ever reaches the network.

The trade-off: control comes with responsibility

Self-custody removes counterparty risk — no exchange can freeze your funds or go bankrupt with your assets — but it also removes the safety net of "forgot my password, please reset it." If you lose your seed phrase and your device, no one, including the wallet provider, can recover your funds for you. See how to store Kaspa safely for practical steps to avoid that.

What this looks like in practice

In a self-custody wallet, "your password" typically unlocks a locally encrypted copy of your keys on that device — it is not a password stored on a server somewhere that can be reset over email. The seed phrase generated when you create the wallet is the actual backup; the password just protects local access to it.

Why it matters for Kaspa specifically

Kaspa's whole design — a fast, decentralized, permissionless BlockDAG (see what is Kaspa) — is undermined if most users end up holding it through a centralized custodian instead of controlling their own keys. A self-custody wallet like KaspaCom Wallet keeps that decentralization meaningful at the individual level.
